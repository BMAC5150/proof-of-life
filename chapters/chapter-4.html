CH. 04
LOC. IRGC Bunker, Tehran
TIME 09:14 IRST

The Operator slept through the night.

The buses were running again. The government had restored internet access.

He took a seat near the back, where no one could sit behind him, and took out his personal phone. He did this every morning.

The bus was the one place he allowed himself the phone at all. Not the apartment, where his mother could read his face, and not the compound ahead, where the trackers at the gate logged every handset and the workstation logged everything after. The half-empty bus on the western highway was the only stretch of his day that belonged to no one who outranked him. He had made the habit without deciding to.

Overnight IRIB state media had broadcast alerts as statements from the Supreme Leader: “Thousands killed by domestic seditionists and international criminals directed by Trump.”

He deleted them.

He then opened an illegal western newsfeed on Reddit.

The news he was avoiding was there.

The Americans had moved a second carrier group into the Gulf. The Israelis had recalled reserve pilots. The US President expressed outrage and issued threats on social media as he always did, because getting social media clicks was now how Washington communicated its policy to its people without asking permission and to its enemies without the inconvenience of diplomacy.

Everyone in Tehran understood what that meant.

A kilometre out, before the compound’s trackers could find it, he powered the phone down and put it away. By the time the bus reached the gate he was the other version of himself.

At the bunker door he held his card to the reader. A green light.

· · ·

He deposited his phone in the secured lockbox. Two were already there. His reality now narrowed to what his military masters allowed and what his colleagues shared.

The Operator had graduated top of his class at Sharif two years ago. Computer science. The degree that was supposed to open the world. In another country it would have. In this one the technology companies had left after the sanctions, or had never come. The brightest graduates from the best technical university in Iran funneled into three destinations: government research, the military, or the slow erosion of freelance work paid in rials that bought less every month.

Not that anyone had given him much of a choice.

The IRGC had resources. The IRGC had problems worth solving. The IRGC had not asked whether he wanted to solve them.

The Technician was at his station.

Two monitors lit, leaning forward with the posture of a man who arrived early because arriving early was the kind of thing that got noticed. He was younger than the Operator by three years. He had entered the program after the sanctions had fully closed the civilian economy to people with his skills. The IRGC was not his second choice. It was his only one. He had made peace with it.

The Operator had not, which gave his enthusiasm a quality the Operator found enviable and faintly exhausting in equal measure.

He looked up.

“You slept.”

“What gave it away.”

“You don’t look like you want to die.”

The Operator set his backpack down and powered up his workstation.

The Technician returned to his screen. The sound of his typing was faster than anyone else’s in the room. Always was. He coded the way some people talked. Fluently, without apparent effort, with a confidence that did not pause to consider whether the next sentence might be wrong.

The Infrastructure Lead was at his usual position facing the network status displays. Three screens. Three locations. Tehran, Karaj, Marzdaran.

He glanced at the Operator, offered the briefest nod, and returned to his work. In the break room he asked about weekends with brief genuine interest. In here his parsimony was professional identity that had long since become personality.

The Cousin was not at his station. Monitors dark. Chair pushed in.

The Operator noticed this without remarking on it.

The Cousin’s schedule ran laterally to theirs.

Intelligence liaison. A separate analysis room. Meetings that intersected with the Handler’s chain in ways the tactical operators did not fully track. He came and went. Nobody tracked his schedule. Nobody was meant to. He had decided that genuine connection inside this institution was not available and would not be safe if it were.

· · ·

The Handler arrived at 9:14.

He came through the corridor with a leather folder under one arm and tea in the other.

He greeted no one.

Older than the rest of them by two decades.

Compact. Careful. The kind of stillness that came from years of observing other people’s reactions rather than producing them. He did not raise his voice. Never needed to. The silence that followed his questions was a tool he had been sharpening since before anyone else in the room had started university.

The desk in his office was positioned with a sightline to every workstation through the open door. Always neat. The neatness was not personality. A behavioral expression of authority in a space where authority was positional and did not require warmth to function.

Today he looked altered. Not rattled. The look of a man whose ground had shifted during a meeting he could not discuss with the people who depended on him.

He opened the folder. Reviewed something. Closed it. Opened it again.

“Daily briefing in ten minutes,” he said without looking up.

The Operator noticed the folder opening twice. He filed it.

· · ·

Four chairs at the table near the Handler’s office. The Handler at the head. The Operator and the Technician facing each other.

The Cousin arrived two minutes before and took the fourth seat without explanation, setting a thin folder in front of him.

The Handler began, and the Operator understood within the first two minutes that this briefing was different.

Current campaign status covered in four sentences. Target coverages acknowledged without discussion. The Handler moved through the tactical items as more of a checklist of decisions he had already made in order to get to something more important.

Then he set his pen down.

“Division 1500 has realigned unit priorities for Q1.”

The room was quiet.

“The command assessment is that the strategic environment has changed. External military pressure is now the primary planning consideration. Air defense coordination, communications resilience, and continuity-of-command infrastructure are receiving the majority of resource allocation for the foreseeable period.”

The Operator translated this in real time.

The Americans are coming. Everyone above us is preparing for war. The money and attention that used to flow toward cyber operations are now flowing toward not being destroyed from the air.

“Our unit’s Q4 output has been assessed as adequate.”

He let the word sit.

Adequate.

Six weeks ago the word would have landed as minor institutional pressure. Today it meant something closer to expendable. In a military apparatus preparing for American and Israeli strikes, adequate meant the people who decided budgets and personnel and protection were looking at this program and asking whether it was worth keeping when the bombs started falling.

“The focus for Q1 requires this unit to demonstrate strategic value that justifies continued resource allocation during a period of competing priorities.”

“The timeline for demonstrating that value is not indefinite.”

Nobody spoke.

They didn’t need to.

The Handler was telling his team that the institution they served was turning its full attention toward survival, and that their corner of it would either prove itself essential or be cannibalized for parts.

The Operator looked at the KPI board.

A new category had appeared on the board since January 9.

Domestic social media monitoring. Hours logged. Accounts flagged. Nobody had asked what the category meant or why it was there or what had happened in the streets that made the state decide its cyber operators should also be watching its own citizens.

His numbers were not trending wrong. They were flat. Flat in a program where the command layer expected growth had always been its own alarm. Now, with the command layer expecting to be bombed, flat meant superfluous.

The Cousin sat with his folder unopened.

Hearing what was said and what was not said and filing both separately.

“The ProxyShell approach,” the Handler said, turning to the Operator. “Your assessment.”

“Functional. Coverage against the current target bank set is approximately sixty percent. The remaining forty require updated credential databases not available without HUMINT support.” A pause. “There is a risk that the new timeline does not account for that dependency.”

The Handler nodded once.

“That dependency is noted.”

He moved on.

The Operator had said what he was permitted to say. The Handler had acknowledged it in the form that permitted acknowledgment.

The Handler closed his folder.

“I will be direct. The Americans and the Israelis are not conducting a military exercise. The command assessment is that strikes on Iranian nuclear and military infrastructure are probable within the coming weeks. This unit’s work will either contribute to the national defense posture in a way that is recognized at the highest levels, or its resources will be redirected to operations that do.”

He stood.

“Our Amaliat-e Afandi on American banks need to be accelerated and enhanced immediately to deliver more financial resources to Division. See to it.”

The briefing was over.

· · ·

The Operator went to the tea alcove and stood there longer than the act of pouring required.

Probable within the coming weeks. He had known. Everyone had known.

Hearing it in the Handler’s flat institutional register meant the information had traveled down from people with access to satellite imagery and signals intercepts.

This was different from reading it on a news site through a VPN.

It meant the people who decided things had decided this was real.

The Technician appeared beside him.

“The secondary authentication bypass is still throwing errors against the updated target configurations.”

The Operator looked at him.

The Technician’s face showed no trace of the briefing that had ended four minutes ago.

He parsed the world into separate containers. War was one container. Code was another.

“If we adjust the timing window for the new Microsoft patch cycle we might achieve sixty percent conversion.”

“Sixty is not enough.”

“More than what we have.”

“What we have is a program that just received a timeline to prove it should exist. Sixty percent of an approach already underperforming is not what that requires.”

The Technician considered this. His enthusiasm did not diminish. It recalibrated.

“Then we need a different approach.”

“Yes.”

The Operator poured his tea and said nothing.

The Technician waited, received nothing further, and went back to his station with the energy of a man who had been given a larger problem and found the upgrade interesting.

· · ·

The afternoon passed.

On the surface the technical work was enough to hold their attention. Underneath they processed the current of what the Handler had said.

The Technician pulled up a legacy Nofouz banking operation API from one of the secondary targets.

“This was written by someone who learned to code from a government manual in 1987.”

The Operator did not smile.

“Give it to me. I will make it sing in this century and for Division.”

There was a quiet beat. A shared recognition. Then back to the screen.

They were more themselves in the argument about code than in any other exchange the room allowed. The technical space was the only unregulated space. In it, two men could be what they actually were. Young. Skilled. Working on problems that interested them inside a structure neither had chosen and neither could leave.

The Infrastructure Lead passed behind them.

“Karaj relay running two minutes latency. Configuration update in progress.”

Complete. Minimal.

· · ·

It was afternoon tea time in the break room.

A corridor with a window at the far end showing a rectangle of the late afternoon Tehran sky. Grey. Cold pressing against the glass. A counter with a kettle, cups, sugar, a dish of dates someone replenished without being asked.

The Operator gazed out the window to avoid looking at the room.

Reza was twenty-six. Eastern districts. A mouth that did not close when closing was the only thing that kept you alive.

That was Reza. Loud when it mattered. Quiet when it didn’t. The smartest person in the applied math lab at Sharif and the worst at pretending not to be. He argued with professors who did not want to be argued with. He laughed in rooms that were not built for laughter.

Their differences made them friends. Their choices changed them both.

The Operator chose the card.

Reza had not chosen the card.

He went into the freelance world instead. The rials. The VPN contracts. The slow bleed the sanctions made of everyone who tried to build something the state did not own.

Reza WhatsApped him a link three weeks ago. Persepolis match highlights. No words. Just the link.

The government-imposed internet blackout came that same night.

The blackout killed the phones before anything else. Nobody in the eastern districts could call out. Nobody outside could call in. For three days Reza existed only in the memories of people who had seen him last.

The link sat in his phone now like an artifact pulled from rubble. Still blue. Untapped.

He was at this window. Reza was not.

The tea burned through the glass. He shifted his grip.

The Cousin entered the room with his thermos cup.

“The Americans.”

The Cousin walked to the counter and reached behind the kettle as if looking for something. His hand stayed there for a second longer than required for finding sugar or cinnamon. The Operator noticed because the Operator noticed everything.

“The command layer will want results that can support retaliation capability. Something they can brief upward as a credible asymmetric response.”

The Operator looked at him.

It was the most analytical thing the Cousin had said in the open room in weeks. And it was exactly right.

The IRGC’s conventional assets could not compete with what the Americans and Israelis were assembling. The regime’s survival depended on demonstrating it could hurt the adversary in domains where carrier groups did not matter. If the cyber program could not deliver something that looked like strategic advantage, it would be stripped for parts.

“Something significant,” the Cousin said. “That was his word.”

The closest the Cousin ever came to commentary. Said with the deniability of a man who knew exactly how much he could say in this building.

“There is always a word.” The Operator nearly smiled.

From the main room, the Technician’s keyboard chattered. Fast. Untroubled.

The Operator stood at the break room door and looked down the corridor toward the grey sky.

Reza was out there. Or was not. He was not going to find out from inside this building.

He went back to his station.

· · ·

The end of the day arrived.

The Technician powered down first. Packed his bag with brisk efficiency. Said his goodnights.

The Infrastructure Lead followed. Network check. Operations log. Phone retrieved. No ceremony.

The Cousin had disappeared. The Operator had not noticed when.

The Handler remained at his desk.

Writing. His pen composing language about his quarterly cyber assessment for people who were thinking about air defense batteries and command bunker survival.

He stopped writing to stare at the words.

He was writing for an audience that had stopped listening.

The Operator stayed at his station. Monitors lit.

The legacy bank hacking code was on his screen. His eyes barely blinked as he stared at the screen while his fingers moved across the keyboard. After 30 minutes he saved the new codebase and uploaded it for final testing and deployment. Tomorrow.

The Handler looked up.

“You are still here.”

“Finishing something.”

He held the gaze. Then returned to his writing. The Operator returned to his screen. Neither was finishing anything.

The work was done. They stayed anyway.

· · ·

The printer activated.

The sound was ordinary. A page feeding through.

The timing was not. With two people in the room, it carried a different weight.

The Handler retrieved the page.

Read it. Read it again.

He sat back down. Opened a second drawer. Retrieved a translation log. Cross-referenced. Set the page flat on his desk with both hands on either side of it.

For a man whose superiors were consumed with carrier group positions and strike packages and whether the Supreme Leader’s command bunker could survive a GBU-57 bomb, the page was an administrative footnote.

A query relayed through a channel he had not thought about in months. From a partnership that ranked nowhere on the priority list.

He almost set it aside.

“Where is country code 58?” he said.

He said it to the room. The room was nearly empty. Only the Operator heard, and the Operator did not know.

From the corridor, footsteps.

The Cousin appeared in the doorway. Returning for something at his station. A jacket. A folder. Something small enough to have been the reason or the excuse.

The Handler looked at him.

“Country code 58. Where.”

“Venezuela,” the Cousin said.

Without hesitation. As if rehearsed.

The Handler looked at the page.

His superiors were not thinking about Venezuela.

His superiors were not thinking about cyber operations at all except as one line item in a defense posture that was rapidly becoming a question of whether the regime would exist by spring. The query from country code 58 was, in the hierarchy of things that mattered to the Islamic Republic of Iran in late January 2026, nothing.

He folded the page once. Placed it inside the leather folder. Stood.

Put on his coat.

Walked past the Operator’s desk without acknowledgment. Past the Cousin in the doorway. Through the corridor toward the steel door.

The green light flashed as the door opened.

Then went dark.

The room held the silence. The hum of the servers. The smell of food that had been warming the air since morning.

The Cousin stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then retrieved what he had come for. Returned his chair to its position. Went to his screen.

The Operator watched him.

He didn’t need to look it up.

The Operator filed this where he filed everything.

He shut down his workstation and began the journey back to the apartment and the life waiting on the other side.

The journey was a return to the civilian self, and the civilian self was carrying more than it had been carrying before January. Before the blackout. Before when Reza stopped answering. Before the new category appeared on the KPI board and everyone logged their hours without asking what it was for.

Behind him, in the dark, the code he had finished sat on the server, queued for tomorrow.
