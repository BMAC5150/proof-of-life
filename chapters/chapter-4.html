CH.04
LOC.Tehran, Iran
TIME09:14 IRST

The Operator slept through the night.

The buses were running again. The government had restored internet access.

He took a seat near the back, where no one could sit behind him, and took out his personal phone. He did this every morning.

The bus was the one place he allowed himself the phone at all. Not the apartment, where his mother could read his face, and not the compound, where the trackers at the gate logged every handset.

Overnight IRIB state media had broadcast alerts as statements from the Supreme Leader: “Thousands killed by domestic seditionists and international criminals directed by Trump.”

He deleted them.

He then opened an illegal western newsfeed on Reddit.

The news he was avoiding was there.

The Americans had moved a second carrier group into the Gulf. The Israelis had recalled reserve pilots. The US President issued threats on social media as he always did.

Everyone in Tehran understood what that meant.

A kilometer out, before the compound’s trackers could find it, he powered the phone down and put it away. By the time the bus reached the gate he was the other version of himself.

At the bunker door he held his card to the reader. A green light.

He deposited his phone in the secured lockbox. Two were already there. His reality now narrowed to what his military masters allowed and what his colleagues shared.

The Operator had graduated top of his class at Sharif two years ago. Computer science. The degree that was supposed to open the world. In another country it would have. In this one the technology companies had left after the sanctions, or had never come. The brightest graduates from the best technical university in Iran funneled into three destinations: government research, the military, or the slow erosion of freelance work paid in rials that bought less every month.

Not that anyone had given him much of a choice.

The IRGC had resources. The IRGC had problems worth solving. The IRGC had not asked whether he wanted to solve them.

The Technician was at his station.

Two monitors lit, leaning forward. He arrived early because arriving early was the kind of thing that got noticed. He was younger than the Operator by three years. He had entered the program after the sanctions had fully closed the civilian economy to people with his skills. The IRGC was not his second choice. It was his only one. He had made peace with it.

The Operator had not, which gave his enthusiasm a quality the Operator found enviable and faintly exhausting in equal measure.

He looked up.

“You slept.”

“What gave it away.”

“You don’t look like you want to die.”

The Operator set his backpack down and powered up his workstation.

The Technician returned to his screen. The sound of his typing was faster than anyone else’s in the room. Always was. He coded the way some people talked. Fluently, without apparent effort, with a confidence that did not pause to consider whether the next sentence might be wrong.

The Infrastructure Lead was at his usual position facing the network status displays. Three screens. Three locations. Tehran, Karaj, Marzdaran.

He glanced at the Operator, offered the briefest nod, and returned to his work. In the break room he asked about weekends with brief genuine interest.

The Liaison was not at his station. Monitors dark. Chair pushed in.

The Operator noticed this without remarking on it.

The Liaison’s schedule ran laterally to theirs.

Intelligence liaison. A separate analysis room. Meetings that intersected with the Handler’s chain in ways the tactical operators did not fully track. He came and went. Nobody tracked his schedule. Nobody was meant to. He had decided that genuine connection inside this institution was not available and would not be safe if it were.

The Handler arrived at 9:14.

He came through the corridor with a leather folder under one arm and tea in the other.

He greeted no one.

Older than the rest of them by two decades.

Compact. Careful. A stillness that came from years of observing other people’s reactions rather than producing them. He did not raise his voice. Never needed to. The silence that followed his questions was a tool he had been sharpening since before anyone else in the room had started university.

The desk in his office was positioned with a sightline to every workstation through the open door. Always neat. He kept it that way so nobody in the room had anywhere else to look when he was talking.

Today he looked altered.

He opened the folder. Reviewed something. Closed it. Opened it again.

“Daily briefing in ten minutes,” he said without looking up.

The Operator noticed the folder opening twice. He filed it.

Four chairs at the table near the Handler’s office. The Handler at the head. The Operator and the Technician facing each other.

The Liaison arrived two minutes before and took the fourth seat without explanation, setting a thin folder in front of him.

The Handler began, and the Operator understood within the first two minutes that this briefing was different.

Current campaign status covered in four sentences. Target coverages acknowledged without discussion. The Handler moved through the tactical items as more of a checklist of decisions he had already made in order to get to something more important.

Then he set his pen down.

“Division 1500 has realigned unit priorities for Q1.”

The room was quiet.

“The command assessment is that the strategic environment has changed. External military pressure is now the primary planning consideration. Air defense coordination, communications resilience, and continuity-of-command infrastructure are receiving the majority of resource allocation for the foreseeable period.”

The Operator translated this in real time.

The Americans are coming. Everyone above us is preparing for war. The money and attention that used to flow toward cyber operations are now flowing toward not being destroyed from the air.

“Our unit’s Q4 output has been assessed as adequate.”

He let the word sit.

Adequate.

Six weeks ago the word would have landed as minor institutional pressure. Today it meant something closer to expendable. In a military apparatus preparing for American and Israeli strikes, adequate meant the people who decided budgets and personnel and protection were looking at this program and asking whether it was worth keeping when the bombs started falling.

“The focus for Q1 requires this unit to demonstrate strategic value that justifies continued resource allocation during a period of competing priorities.”

“The timeline for demonstrating that value is not indefinite.”

Nobody spoke.

The Operator looked at the KPI board.

A new category had appeared on the board since January 9.

Domestic social media monitoring. Hours logged. Accounts flagged. Nobody had asked what the category meant or why it was there or what had happened in the streets that made the state decide its cyber operators should also be watching its own citizens.

The Liaison sat with his folder unopened.

Hearing what was said and what was not said and filing both separately.

“The ProxyShell approach,” the Handler said, turning to the Operator. “Your assessment.”

“Functional. Coverage against the current target bank set is approximately sixty percent. The remaining forty require updated credential databases not available without HUMINT support.” A pause. “There is a risk that the new timeline does not account for that dependency.”

The Handler nodded once.

“That dependency is noted.”

He moved on.

The Handler closed his folder.

“I will be direct. The Americans and the Israelis are not conducting a military exercise. The command assessment is that strikes on Iranian nuclear and military infrastructure are probable within the coming weeks. This unit’s work will either contribute to the national defense posture and be recognized at the highest levels, or its resources will be redirected to operations that do.”

He stood.

“Our Amaliat-e Afandi on American banks need to be accelerated and enhanced immediately to deliver more financial resources to Division. See to it.”

The briefing was over.

The Operator went to the tea alcove and stood there longer than the act of pouring required.

Probable within the coming weeks. He had known. Everyone had known.

It meant the people who decided things had decided this was real.

The Technician appeared beside him.

“The secondary authentication bypass is still throwing errors against the updated target configurations.”

The Operator looked at him.

The Technician’s face showed no trace of the briefing that had ended four minutes ago.

He parsed the world into separate containers. War was one container. Code was another.

“If we adjust the timing window for the new Microsoft patch cycle we might achieve sixty percent conversion.”

“Sixty is not enough.”

“More than what we have.”

“What we have is a program that just received a timeline to prove it should exist. Sixty percent of an approach already underperforming is not what that requires.”

The Technician considered this. His enthusiasm did not diminish. It recalibrated.

“Then we need a different approach.”

“Yes.”

The Operator poured his tea and said nothing.

The Technician waited, received nothing further, and went back to his station with the energy of a man who had been given a larger problem and found the upgrade interesting.

The afternoon passed.

The Technician pulled up a legacy Nofouz banking operation API from one of the secondary targets.

“This was written by someone who learned to code from a government manual in 1987.”

The Operator did not smile.

“Give it to me. I will make it sing in this century and for Division.”

There was a quiet beat. A shared recognition. Then back to the screen.

For a few minutes, the code was the only thing in the room. Young. Skilled. Working on problems that interested them inside a structure neither had chosen and neither could leave.

The Infrastructure Lead passed behind them.

“Karaj relay running two minutes latency. Configuration update in progress.”

Complete. Minimal.

It was afternoon tea time in the break room.

A corridor with a window at the far end showing a rectangle of the late afternoon Tehran sky. Gray. Cold pressing against the glass. A counter with a kettle, cups, sugar, a dish of dates someone replenished without being asked.

The Operator gazed out the window to avoid looking at the room.

Reza was twenty-six. Eastern districts. A mouth that did not close when closing was the only thing that kept you alive.

That was Reza. Loud when it mattered. Quiet when it didn’t. The smartest person in the applied math lab at Sharif and the worst at pretending not to be. He argued with professors who did not want to be argued with. He laughed in rooms that were not built for laughter.

Their differences made them friends. Their choices changed them both.

The Operator chose the card.

Reza had not chosen the card.

He went into the freelance world instead. The rials. The VPN contracts. The slow bleed the sanctions made of everyone who tried to build something the state did not own.

Reza WhatsApped him a link three weeks ago. Persepolis match highlights. No words. Just the link.

The government-imposed internet blackout came that same night.

The blackout killed the phones before anything else. Nobody in the eastern districts could call out. Nobody outside could call in. For three days Reza existed only in the memories of people who had seen him last.

The link sat in his phone now like an artifact pulled from rubble. Still blue. Untapped.

He was at this window. Reza was not.

The tea burned through the glass. He shifted his grip.

The Liaison entered the room with his thermos cup.

“The Americans.”

The Liaison walked to the counter and reached behind the kettle as if looking for something. His hand stayed there for a second longer than required for finding sugar or cinnamon.

“The command layer will want results that can support retaliation capability. Something they can brief upward as a credible asymmetric response.”

The Operator looked at him.

It was the most analytical thing the Liaison had said in the open room in weeks. And it was exactly right.

“Something significant,” the Liaison said. “That was his word.”

“There is always a word.” The Operator nearly smiled.

From the main room, the Technician’s keyboard chattered. Fast. Untroubled.

The Operator stood at the break room door and looked down the corridor toward the gray sky.

Reza was out there. Or was not. He was not going to find out from inside this building.

He went back to his station.

The end of the day arrived.

The others left one by one. The Liaison had disappeared. The Operator had not noticed when.

The Handler remained in his office at his desk.

Writing. His pen composing language about his quarterly cyber assessment for people who were thinking about air defense batteries and command bunker survival.

He stopped writing to stare at the words.

He was writing for an audience that had stopped listening.

The Operator stayed at his station. Monitors lit.

The legacy bank hacking code was on his screen. His eyes barely blinked as he stared at the screen while his fingers moved across the keyboard. After 30 minutes he saved the new codebase and uploaded it for final testing and deployment. Tomorrow.

The Handler looked up and called through the office door.

“You are still here.”

“Finishing something.”

He held the gaze. Then returned to his writing. The Operator returned to his screen.

The work was done. They stayed anyway.

The printer activated.

The sound was ordinary. A page feeding through.

The timing was not.

The Handler emerged from his office to retrieve the page.

Read it. Read it again.

He returned to his office. Opened a second drawer. Retrieved a translation log. Cross-referenced. Set the page flat on his desk with both hands on either side of it.

For a man whose superiors were consumed with carrier group positions and strike packages and whether the Supreme Leader’s command bunker could survive a GBU-57 bomb, the page was an administrative footnote.

A query relayed through a channel he had not thought about in months. From a partnership that ranked nowhere on the priority list.

He almost set it aside.

“Where is country code 58?” he said.

He said it to the room. The room was nearly empty. Only the Operator heard, and the Operator did not know.

From the corridor, footsteps.

The Liaison appeared in the doorway. Returning for something at his station. A jacket. A folder. Something small enough to have been the reason or the excuse.

The Handler looked at him.

“Country code 58. Where.”

“Venezuela,” the Liaison said.

Without hesitation. As if rehearsed.

The Handler looked at the page.

His superiors were not thinking about Venezuela.

His superiors were not thinking about cyber operations at all except as one line item in a defense posture that was rapidly becoming a question of whether the regime would exist by spring. The query from country code 58 was, in the hierarchy of things that mattered to the Islamic Republic of Iran in early February 2026, nothing.

He folded the page once. Placed it inside the leather folder. Stood.

Put on his coat.

Walked past the Operator’s desk without acknowledgment. Past the Liaison in the doorway. Through the corridor toward the steel door.

The green light flashed as the door opened.

Then went dark.

The silence held. The hum of the servers. The smell of food that had been warming the air since morning.

The Liaison stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then retrieved what he had come for. Returned his chair to its position. Went to his screen.

The Operator watched him.

He didn’t need to look it up.

The Operator kept this the way he kept everything else he didn’t say out loud.

He shut down his workstation and began the journey back to the apartment and the life waiting on the other side.

Behind him, in the dark, the code he had finished sat on the server, queued for tomorrow.

Chapter Four-A — IRGC Bunker, Outside Tehran · 16:34 IRST

The Operator arrived at the bunker earlier than normal the next morning.

He spent most of it testing and retesting the new code and configuring it against the identified target list.

At 16:34 the Handler approved the operation for launch.

The room had been running hard all day and would run into the night when the American banks across the ocean closed their business day.

Outside the bunker it was full dark, the Tehran winter evening pressing cold against the buried walls. Inside, the air was the same air it always was. Warm electronics. Cooling systems. The saffron-and-turmeric smell of food gone cold in its containers since noon, because no one had stopped to eat it.

The Technician ran the center station.

He had been at it since the briefing, leaning into his two monitors, and when the work went well he spoke to the screen in a low half-voice that no one was meant to answer.

The operation was going well.

A session stood open on his right monitor. The website of Cumberland Mutual Savings. A customer signing in to look at his savings account, the way he or she probably did most mornings.

It was not the customer.

The customer was a retiree in a small American city, eight and a half hours behind them with no idea anyone was inside the account. The man signing in was the Technician, in a buried room outside Tehran, pretending to be him or her. The bank could not tell the two of them apart.

The bank saw everything it had ever asked a customer to prove. A trusted laptop. A session already signed in.

The Operator had spent eleven weeks building the target list across multiple banks.

Sixty out of every hundred. That had been the performance ceiling for a year. The other forty needed credential dumps the Handler kept promising and never delivered.

Adequate.

Yesterday the Operator stopped waiting for the forty and built a different way in.

The new code did not need the password, and it did not need to beat the bank’s second check, because it never met it. It went in through the bank’s own account-recovery path, the one built for customers who had lost everything, and let the bank vouch for him. An account he had never held a password for became an account he owned, in under a minute, with no one handing him anything.

Today sixty became ninety-four.

And the forty he had been locked out of turned out to be the richest of them. The careful ones. The people who never reused a password were the same people who had the most to guard, which made them the most to take.

Fifty-three of them tonight across seven different banks, where a year ago the same rule would have given him barely thirty. He read down the column.

The system gave him the names the short way, last name and an initial. Halloran, R. And a number. Three hundred and one thousand, two hundred and fifty-six.

He knew that the name was a person, a home somewhere with furniture and pictures. He looked at the word beside it, the one that meant the account was open and the money was theirs to take, and when it turned green he would say, “Next.”

“Wire’s away,” the Technician said. “The bank pushed it out itself. As far as Cumberland knows, he asked them to.”

From the wall station the Infrastructure Lead spoke without turning. “Exit’s holding. Marzdaran to the first hop, first hop out. Under a second.”

He watched a relay he did not trust drop back inside tolerance, and under his breath, to the equipment and to no one, he said, “Khoda ro shokr.”

“Next,” the Operator said.

The Technician did not open the next account. He pulled something else onto his second monitor instead.

“Checking he’s not on-line,” he said. “If he’s mid-session on his end, the app flags it and the wire holds for review.”

The Operator looked over at the window that came up on the second screen.

A live camera image. Grainy and gray, a wide bowl of a room bent at its edges by the lens, the picture catching up to itself in steps the way the cheap ones did. A kitchen behind, a light on over the sink.

A man sat at a desk in the middle of the room in a wheelchair. A bulky old PC box and monitor on the desk.

Behind him a shelf held things the operators took in as shapes and let go. A helmet. A photograph of men in heavy coats standing in a row. The man was broad through the shoulders and gone gray, and his legs did not move when his hands did.

He was looking at the screen the camera could not see.

Then he stopped looking at it.

He put his hands to the wheels and rolled the chair back. He opened the bottom drawer of the desk and took out a revolver. He held it in his lap a moment. Then he put the barrel under his chin with both hands and fired.

The feed had no sound. The camera’s picture shuddered, the way the cheap ones did, and caught up to itself half a second late. The chair had rolled back into the middle of the room. The man in it had slid down and to one side. His hands fell open. The revolver hit the floor.

On the shelf behind him the helmet and the photograph of men in heavy coats stayed exactly where they were.

He did not move again.

The feed ran on. Gray. Silent. The light still on over the sink.

The Technician watched a second longer.

“Close it,” the Operator said.

The Technician closed it.

On the other monitor the wire had cleared while they watched. The money was gone and the man was in the chair, and the two facts sat a hand’s width apart.

In the bunker the field went green and a tone sounded and the next session opened. By the time the Technician sat back, the cohort was finished.

“Fifty-one for fifty-three,” he said. He rolled his neck. He reached for the tea that had gone cold an hour ago and drank it. There was nothing on his face but the clean pleasure of a hard job done right. “Nine million one. A little over.”

“Stage it out,” the Operator said.

The Infrastructure Lead was already doing it, walking the money through a sequence of hops and conversions, each one defensible somewhere, the whole of it folding into a shape a forensic accountant could pick at for a year without ever laying it flat.

The Liaison came through from the analysis room while the money was moving. He did not look at the screens. He crossed to the far door with a folder under his arm and he was gone before the Operator had finished noticing him. No one asked where he’d been. No one ever did.

The Operator watched the money go and felt what he was allowed to feel, which was that the work was good. He knew how good. He had spent eleven weeks on an institution that believed it was safe, and it had never once seen him, and the money was now leaving on rails he had laid by hand.

The Handler came down from his desk at 18:40.

He did not ask for the report. He stood behind the Technician and read the screens himself, slowly, and the men at their desks arranged themselves around his stillness.

“Fifty-one,” he said.

“Nine point one, staged and moving. No flags on their side yet. It’ll be a while before they understand what they’re looking at.”

The Handler set a hand on the back of the Technician’s chair. He looked at the green fields and the cleared list with a pleasure that was not staged.

“Âfarin,” he said.

The Technician lifted his head and smiled.

The Handler let him have the moment, and then took it back.

“It’s clean. It’s elegant. It will brief well.”

He straightened, and the warmth went back behind the careful face and turned into something with a ledger in it. “And in the morning there are fifty-one people on telephones, and an institution opening a file. We spent infrastructure to take nine million dollars from people who keep their savings in one account. We can do it twice more, perhaps, before they learn our approach.”

He turned for the steps to his desk.

“Show the command something they have not seen before, and they will not strip this room for parts when the strikes come.” He said it without looking at the Operator. “This is proof you can cut the key. It is not the lock they are dreaming about.”

He went into his office and sat with the door open and began again to write his assessment for men whose attention was on carrier groups and command bunkers.

The Technician sat a moment and stared at nothing. After a minute he began to write up the operation for the brief. He had already reset around the larger problem, wondering what the lock looked like.

The Operator powered down his station.

He pulled his coat off the back of the chair and fit the uneaten lunch his mother had packed for him into his backpack.

The indicator light at the steel door shifted from green to red as he walked out into the cold of a city that was waiting for a war, carrying his mother’s bread and nine million dollars he would never touch and a number, three hundred and one thousand, two hundred and fifty-six, that he had already stopped seeing.

Chapter Four-B — Johnstown, PA · 10:09 EST

The call came in at 10:09 that morning.

A welfare check. East Side, Johnstown.

A woman two doors down had heard it through the brick and the February cold and called it in.

Engine 4 was nine blocks out.

The men on it had worked for Captain Ray Halloran. Fifteen years. Before the Iron Street roof. Before the chair.

They came up the porch steps fast.

Then they stopped.

They knew the room. The scanner on the sill. The coffee he made too strong. The shelf. The company photo. The commendation from Iron Street, framed. Two kids off a third floor. His legs were the price.

The camera on top of the TV. The desk where he paid the bills to the dollar. The PC with the Green Bay Packers mousepad.

They had carried strangers down ladders out of black smoke. There was nothing here for their hands to do.

One of them took off his helmet.

The screen showed the account entries for Ray Halloran: Opening balance: $301,256.33 Withdrawals: $301,256.33 Closing balance: $0.00

The pension. The Iron Street settlement. The careful rest of it. Gone by wire transfer that morning. Minutes before the call.

The bank’s record on the screen showed Ray Halloran sent it himself. Signed in as himself. Confirmed from his own phone.

The fire report would say one thing.

The medical examiner would say another.

The bank would say a third.

Each without mentioning the man who spent thirty years running into what everyone else ran from.

Each in the only language it had for He did it to himself.
