CH.10
LOC.Tehran, Iran
TIMEMorning IRST

The Trump post came through during the bus ride to the bunker.

A photograph of a carrier group crossing into the Arabian Sea. Peace through Strength!

Below it, the second post threatening the energy infrastructure if the regime killed more protesters. He had been making that threat since the protests began. The regime had killed thousands since. The infrastructure was still standing.

The Operator put the phone away.

Promises made but not kept.

By the time he reached the compound the sun was up. Same packed dirt, same low buildings, same nothing about the place that suggested importance.

He held his card to the reader. Green.

The outside world closed behind him.

The Operator deposited his phone in the secured lockbox. Two phones already in their proper places.

Kaveh Mohseni noted his colleagues’ presence in the bunker and moved toward his workstation.

The Technician was at his station, head swiveling from one screen to another when a display changed unexpectedly.

Mehdi Ahangari was the youngest in the room and the first to arrive every morning. He possessed boundless energy where the others had gone quiet, with skills the Handler had already learned to use.

He looked up.

“Kaveh.”

The Operator set his backpack down.

“Anything, Mehdi?”

“Karaj relay is clean. Running a patch cycle on the secondary monitoring stack. Nothing interesting.”

The Operator powered up his workstation.

The Technician’s typing resumed. Fast. Always fast. His keystrokes finding a rhythm to blend in with the humming of the air conditioner.

The Infrastructure Lead sat in his usual position facing the network status displays.

Farhad Kazemi had worked in this room longer than anyone except the Handler, and somewhere in those years his silence had stopped being a choice and become the man. He glanced at the Operator, the briefest nod, and went back to his screens.

The Liaison’s station was dark. Monitors off. Chair pushed in.

“No Sohrab yet.” The Operator said it to the room. The room did not answer.

The room had changed since the Division 1500 briefing. Not in any visible way. Everyone had absorbed the directive and gone back to work because going back to work was the only available response.

The Handler came through the corridor at nine-twelve, leather folder under one arm, and walked past his own office.

Javad Ghorbani. Twenty-five years in this building or buildings like it. Twenty-five years of reading other people’s reactions rather than producing them. His clothes were always clean, crisply ironed. It was authority expressed as impeccable grooming.

Today something was different. He did not go to his desk.

The Liaison had arrived sometime in the last few minutes. The Operator had not heard him come in, which was not unusual.

Sohrab Rostami moved through the building quietly and deliberately as if he had decided years ago that being noticed was not to his advantage. He was at his workstation now, monitors on, sorting something.

The Handler walked past the Operator’s station.

He set a small black thumb drive on the Liaison’s desk without ceremony.

“Venezuelan request. Financial technology company. American. There is a venture capital firm in New York brokering the relationship. The materials include their analyst’s assessment.”

He looked at the Liaison. “You have diaspora context for this region. The analyst’s background will be familiar to you. That is why this is on your desk and not Kaveh’s. Assess and advise. Don’t let it eat your week.”

The Handler turned and walked back toward his office.

The thumb drive sat on the Liaison’s desk. Small. Black. Unremarkable.

The Operator watched the Handler disappear behind his office door followed by the muffled voice of a call begun with Division.

He saw the Liaison look at the thumb drive. He filed both observations and returned to his screen.

The Liaison plugged the drive into the air-gapped review workstation. Standard protocol for externally sourced media. He began working through the contents.

Four items.

A presentation file. Thirty-four slides. Prepared by a venture capital firm called Prescott Calder.

A technical architecture summary. Twelve pages. Internal document from a company called Sovren.

Analyst’s notes. Eight pages. The VC’s internal analysis.

A meeting confirmation. Calendar invitation showing a date seven days from now, an attendee list, and an office address in New York. 902 Broadway.

He read the analyst’s notes first.

This was behavioral, not random. His training started with personnel. Who wrote this. What do they know. What does their position tell you about the chain.

He saw the name.

Anahita Rostami. Senior Analyst, Prescott Calder.

He read it. He read it again.

He set the page down.

The room did not change. Nobody looked up.

Sohrab Rostami’s face did not change either. What changed was the posture of his stillness. The held breath that was not about intelligence analysis. His uncle’s daughter. His father had said her name once, years ago, and not again. He had never met her.

The Cousin continued processing.

He routed the thumb drive’s contents to the Operator’s queue with a standard intake flag: “Venezuelan-sourced. Financial technology platform. Possible utility assessment requested.”

Complete.

He did not flag the Analyst’s name. Not in the routing note.

A quiet exhale. A forced swallow.

He returned to the thumb drive materials at his own station and continued reading with the discipline of a man who had just found something he would not share.

The Operator received the materials in his queue during a break between primary campaign tasks. He opened them the way he opened everything from this channel. Without expectation. This channel had never produced anything consequential.

He read the presentation file first.

Standard venture capital formatting. Product overview, market opportunity, competitive landscape, team. He read it quickly. Discarding the marketing language. Looking for the structural logic underneath.

He opened the technical architecture summary next. Twelve pages.

He read through the first six pages at pace. Competent. Well-designed. The kind of clean engineering that meant someone good had built this.

He took a sip of tea at page seven. The AIRC module specification.

His reading speed changed.

The Technician, if he had been watching, would have recognized the shift.

He stopped reading at page eleven.

He read it again. Then a third time.

The lean forward. The slight narrowing of focus, involuntary and total. Analysis had replaced processing.

He opened a blank document on the air-gapped system and began making notes. Working through the architecture’s implications. What the commercial pitch described. What it did not describe. The gap between the stated function and the structural capability.

The AIRC module’s behavioral inference engine was designed to detect anomalies in financial transaction patterns.

A validation tool. A monitoring system. But the architecture allowed inversion. The same engine that identified unusual behavior could be used to operate within the behavioral envelope the model defined as normal. Every action masked. Every movement invisible to monitoring systems. Because the system understood what “normal” looked like and could perform it perfectly.

This was not a data hygiene tool.

This was a way to move through banking infrastructure without being seen. A financial ghost. A tool that could operate inside the transaction systems of any institution the platform connected to, and the platform already connected to dozens.

It could map transactions. It could manipulate identities. It could also destroy them.

The Operator sat with this for fifty-one minutes, running the inversion model against three LATAM banking regulatory frameworks he had studied. The logic held in every one.

This had serious money potential. Real money.

He opened the analyst’s notes. Anahita Rostami’s analysis. He read the first two pages and stopped.

Anahita Rostami.

He looked across the room. The Cousin was bent over his own screen and did not look up.

Rostami was a common name. There had been two in his year at Sharif.

Her reading of the AIRC architecture was precise, technically literate, and arrived at several of the implications he had been working through independently. She had seen the capability layers. She had noted the edge cases. She had done, in eight pages of analyst notes, the same work he had done in just over fifty minutes of private analysis.

She was very good.

He closed the laptop.

He opened his own workstation. He began his analysis from scratch. From the architecture summary and the pitch deck alone. As if her work did not exist.

His independent analysis arrived at the same conclusions she had reached.

The Operator walked to the Handler’s office doorway.

The Handler was on a call. Something about air defense resource priorities. The Operator waited. He stood in the doorway knowing that this conversation would rearrange the rest of his day.

The Handler looked up.

“Kaveh. Come.” Part recognition. Part summons.

“The Venezuelan material. There may be something substantial here. I cannot be certain without access to the codebase.”

The Handler studied him. He had spent twenty-five years reading controlled tones.

“Give them only adequate. Don’t fall behind on your numbers. If you need the code, figure out how to get it.”

The conversation was over. The Handler returned to his screen.

The Operator left.

He did not look at the Cousin as he crossed the room. The Cousin had been at his station since routing the materials. He had not turned a page in some time.

The Operator went to the Technician’s station early in the afternoon.

“We need access to their codebase repository to complete our assessment. We have seven days before this meeting happens.”

The Technician knew what had to be done.

He studied the Sovren attendee list from the calendar invitation. Four names. He grinned at the Operator.

“Which one?”

“Go find out, Mehdi.”

The Technician’s energy shifted to genuine.

He had been given a targeting problem, and targeting problems were an opportunity to demonstrate his skills. The solutions were technical. And the technical work was absorbing enough to fill the space where other considerations might otherwise sit.

The Technician ran point. The Cousin assisted from his station, pulling OSINT across platforms with the quiet efficiency the cell had come to expect from him.

Collaboration was rare in the bunker.

Walker first. Nothing. No Instagram, no public accounts with usable metadata, no digital footprint worth pursuing. “Hard target,” the Technician said. “Moving on.” One name crossed off the list. Not a person. An item. Okafor: LinkedIn, a private GitHub, no device signatures. “Another wall.” Voss, the third name: a PhD in applied mathematics, a decade of publications, no social media, no personal device signatures. She existed online because academia insisted she be. The Technician closed the tab and moved on.

“What about the analyst?” the Technician said.

The Operator looked up from his station. The Cousin moved away from his keyboard.

“Rostami,” the Technician continued. “She is on the attendee list. She works at the fund. She would have access to their systems through the diligence process.”

He pulled her up. LinkedIn was professional and curated. No personal social media visible under her professional name. No conference photos with device metadata. iPhone user based on a single image artifact from a tagged photo on a colleague’s account.

“Wait,” the Technician said. “iPhone.” He said it the way a locksmith said hardened steel. Respectfully. The tool he had was not designed for that material.

“She is also not a developer,” the Operator said from across the room. “She would have access to pitch materials and financial documentation. Not the codebase. Not the repository.”

The Cousin looked at the Operator. Eyes frozen.

“Move on,” the Operator said.

Matt Gorski. Co-founder. Technical lead.

LinkedIn active. Conference participation listed. GitHub active. Public commits. Recent activity. Last commit pushed at 11:43 PM EST three days prior. Late-night work pattern confirmed.

Then the Technician found the Instagram.

The data lined up with the target profile.

Active personal account. Conference photos. Workspace images. The kind of social media presence that a young engineer maintained because everyone did, because visibility was currency, because the alternative was invisibility in an economy that did not reward it.

“Four posts in the last six weeks with unstripped EXIF metadata.” The Technician’s voice carried the particular satisfaction of a man who had found what he was looking for. “Device manufacturer: Samsung. Model: Samsung Galaxy S24+. Android confirmed.”

He kept going. Email pattern identified from standard naming convention, confirmed through a LinkedIn message preview artifact.

“Phone number.” The Technician pulled up a cached page. “TechCrunch article from 2023. Journalist contact number appears in the metadata. The live version has been scrubbed.” He looked up from the screen. “The archived version had not been. Archive.org.”

He announced each finding to the room. He was solving a puzzle that was yielding beautifully to his approach. He processed the components of the operation that would compromise a specific person as data points. Clean. Sequential.

“Primary target,” the Operator confirmed from across the room. “Gorski is the vector.”

They worked into the evening.

The lure took shape through short exchanges and technical decisions, an approach they had built many times before against a target they had never had reason to approach.

The Technician identified Prescott Calder’s building. 902 Broadway.

He found the visitor management system through a job posting on the building management company’s website. Envoy. Standard corporate platform. SMS-based pre-registration links.

The plan formed through elimination.

The Technician would clone the Envoy pre-registration page on a lookalike domain with a real TLS certificate. The padlock would show. The page would look right.

Forty-eight hours after the legitimate Envoy text, Gorski would receive a second message: same tone, same structure, sent from a plausible long code. He would tap it because he had been expecting it.

The malware exploit would execute inside his already-trusted browser context in the four seconds it took the page to render. Replayable authenticated state, captured. A single HTTPS exfiltration to the Frankfurt dead-drop.

Then nothing. No persistent implant. A session in Sovren’s logs that looked like a developer checking his own code from a European VPN.

The Operator contributed an exploit chain he had been developing for eighteen months. Two unpatched vulnerabilities in Android’s browser layer. Not in any public database. No visible behavior change. The capability the KPI board had no column for.

“Frankfurt server status, Farhad.” The Operator asked, if for no other reason to ensure his contribution would not fail because of someone else’s sloppiness.

“Clean.”

He delivered this in the minimal register the room had come to expect.

He returned to his station.

The Operator named the operation at the end of the design session. Protocol.

شاهد ساکت

Shahid-e Saket. Silent Witness.

The Technician logged it without remark.

He printed his final assessment from the air-gapped system and carried it to the Handler’s office. He set it on the desk without discussion. The Handler read the first page. Read the second. Set it down.

“Is this everything?”

The Technician stood straighter.

“Full targeting profile. Device signature, email pattern, phone number recovery, social media mapping. The clone page architecture is on page three.”

The Handler’s face showed him a disinterested look.

“The data was lying on the ground, Mehdi. An Instagram account with unstripped metadata. A cached phone number from a three-year-old article. This is collection, not intelligence.” He tapped the page. “I need the lure operational by Wednesday. Not Thursday. Wednesday. Can you do that or should I give it to Kaveh?”

The Technician’s face went still. The energy that had been carrying him since the afternoon collapsed into a single point behind his eyes.

“Wednesday.”

“Good. Go home.”

· · ·

The room emptied the way the bunker always emptied.

The Infrastructure Lead first. Network check, operations log, phone retrieved. No ceremony.

The Technician next. Looking at the floor in front of him.

He walked past the Operator’s station without speaking. He did not say the clone page would be ready by Thursday. His bag was on his shoulder and his face was the face of a twenty-four-year-old who had just been told that the best work of his professional life was not good enough by the only person in the building whose approval he needed.

The Cousin was not at his station. The Operator had not noticed when he left or where he went. He never did.

Something had registered with the Operator.

The Cousin’s rhythm had been off.

The Operator set it aside. It matched nothing else he was tracking.

The Operator and the Handler were the last two in the room.

Two men who stayed because leaving meant returning to a different version of themselves.

The Handler was at his desk.

Writing. Something else. The framework summary he would take upstairs to Division 1500. The document that translated what the Operator had found in the thumb drive into the institutional language that determined whether the program survived. He was writing for an audience that would listen this time. Because he finally had something worth hearing.

The Operator was at his station.

Monitors lit.

Twelve hours ago the Venezuelan materials had been a low-priority evaluation task from a channel that had never produced anything consequential. Now there was an exploitation operation taking shape.

A target selected. A lure designed. A capability he had built over eighteen months was about to be deployed against people who had built something he recognized as extraordinary. Something that might save their bunker. Maybe more.

He stopped reading his monitors. He was thinking past them.

The Handler looked up.

“You are still here.”

“Finishing something.”

The Operator left first.

Still no sign of the Cousin. No jacket on his chair. Phone still in the lockbox. The Operator filed it away.

The bunker door. The green light. The compound. The cold.

He turned back. The green light was still on. The Handler was still inside. Still writing. The light did not go dark the way it had the last time. The room was still running.

The bus headed east. Home. Tehran reassembling itself in the February dark.

He thought about the people who had built the thing on his screen. The architecture was clean. That was what he had noticed first, before anything else.

Tomorrow he would begin the work of getting inside it. He was good at that work. It was the only work the building had ever rewarded him for.
