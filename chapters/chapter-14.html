CH.14
LOC.Greenwich, Connecticut
TIMENight EST

February had not changed its mind about Connecticut at night.

The glow from the desk lamp cast shadows on the portrait-adorned walls of his home office.

Twenty minutes ago his phone had rung.

“Jonathon Victor” on the display. The same careful English. The same greeting.

“Anderson.”

The commitment would increase. The terms would improve. The representative would attend the presentation as discussed. He wants the presentation to address some key technical details.

The call ended. Two short tones. The same two short tones as last time.

The former Senator looked at his phone and found Whitfield Calder in his contacts.

Three rings.

“Senator.”

“Good news, Whit. Our Latin American LP is moving forward. Larger commitment. Better terms.”

He could hear it through the phone. The shift. Calder processing opportunity. Fast. Without friction.

“He wants technical depth,” the former Senator said. “Architecture level. The product. The LATAM markets. Whatever information you have.”

“On it. First thing.”

“Good. See to it.”

He set the phone down on the desk beside the tumbler.

White House crystal, engraved with the seal. A dinner during his third term. The whiskey had been poured before the first call. The ice had melted.

With the monogrammed pen he drew a line through the item on the legal pad. Two more items remained.

He drank the whiskey. Room temperature now.

He did not know what the start-up was called or what they did.

He never asked.

He put the empty tumbler down on the desk.

· · ·

The corner suite was lit when she arrived.

The light through the 14th-floor windows was thin and white.

She set down her bag. Opened her laptop.

The Sovren data room was already loaded from last night’s session. She had been working the presentation framework until eleven. The deal mechanics. The valuation bridge. The questions Calder would want answered before the room was live.

She was good at this. Calder had told her in her first week that the most valuable thing an analyst could do was know what the partner would ask before the partner knew he was going to ask it. She had taken that further. Page eleven had been one of those.

At 9:14 Calder appeared at her desk.

He had come from the corner suite with his phone in his hand. She recognized the energy of deal acceleration. The slight forward lean. The voice half a register higher than his baseline.

“We got a call last night,” he said. “Alejandro is increasing his commitment. Significantly. The terms are going to be more favorable than the first conversation.”

She looked at him.

“How much more.”

“Enough to change the structure of the round. He’s serious, Anahita. This isn’t exploratory anymore.”

He set his phone on the edge of her desk. The Patek Philippe caught the thin light from the window.

“The presentation needs to go deeper than what we’ve been planning. He wants architecture level. Technical depth. How the platform works inside LATAM banking systems, not just the market thesis.”

“That’s beyond a standard LP review.”

“He’s not a standard LP. We discussed that.”

“We discussed that he wanted to understand the product. This sounds like he wants to understand the engineering.”

Calder crossed his arms. A large man comfortable with the space he occupied.

“I spoke with Prescott last night. The Senator is confident in the relationship. He’s known Alejandro’s network for years. This is how these LATAM investors operate. They go deep because they commit deep. It’s a feature, not a flag.”

She heard it. The sentence that was also a door closing.

The Senator is confident in the relationship.

“Has the firm run a standard background on him?”

She said it evenly.

Calder looked at her. The look lasted one second longer than his rhythm usually allowed.

“Prescott brought the relationship. The reference is Prescott. I don’t think I need to explain to you what a twenty-two-year Senator’s referral means in terms of due diligence.”

He smiled. Not coldly. Warmly.

“If Anderson Prescott says the man is good for it, the man is good for it. That’s how this works.”

He did not pick up his phone. He left it on her desk. He looked at her.

“Let me be clear about something.” The warmth was gone.

“You do not go around me on this. You do not go around Prescott. You do not make calls to compliance or anyone else about the provenance of an LP that the founder of this firm personally introduced. If you do that, you are not questioning the deal. You are questioning Anderson Prescott. And I promise you, Anahita, if that happens, you will not work at this firm by the end of the week.”

Then the warmth came back. Like a light turned on. The same smile. The same voice.

“But that’s not going to happen. Because you’re smart. And you know how this works.”

He picked up his phone from her desk.

“Get Walker on the phone this morning. Let them know the scope has expanded because the potential investment is greater. I want the Sovren team ready for technical questions they haven’t been asked before. Architecture. The banking integration layer. All of it.”

“I’ll reach out.”

“Good. This is happening, Anahita. This is real money.”

He was halfway to his office. He turned back.

“One more thing. Draft the IC onboarding memo for Garza. Standard format. I need your sign-off on the KYC certification by end of day.”

“We haven’t completed KYC on him. I don’t have a verified identity. I don’t have a firm name.”

“The Senator’s referral is the verification. Put Prescott’s name in the reference line. That’s the process.”

“That’s not what the form asks for.”

“It’s what I’m asking for. See to it.”

He held her eyes for one beat. Then he went back to the corner suite.

She sat at her desk.

Her right hand was holding the pen she had picked up without noticing. She had been holding it through the second half of the conversation. She looked at her knuckles. White. She set the pen down and opened her hand.

She turned back to her laptop and typed an email to Walker.

Specific technical information to be covered at the presentation and to prepare accordingly. A reminder not a request.

She hit send.

Page eleven. She had read it four times now. She had put it on the file once, with her name on it, and been told to take it off. She had not raised it in a meeting.

She opened a new browser tab. Typed Alejandro Garza LATAM banking and financial services.

The results came back in less than a second.

Hundreds of them. An Alejandro Garza in private equity in Mexico City. Another in agricultural commodities in Buenos Aires. A retired banker in São Paulo.

She scrolled.

None of them resolved. None of them connected to the man Calder had described. No firm name she could cross-reference. No portfolio she could verify. No conference appearance, no panel, no published quote. Just a name shared by dozens of men across a continent, any one of whom could be the person restructuring the round and none of whom she could confirm.

She stared at the screen.

She had been in this building for two and a half years. She had processed twenty-three deals through this office. She had never once evaluated a deal for an investor whose identity dissolved into a search result.

She could ask Calder for more. A firm name. A city. A LinkedIn profile she could match. That was the kind of question that had a form. A due diligence documentation process.

She did not ask.

She closed the tab.

She opened the presentation framework instead and began rebuilding the question architecture for the deeper scope Calder had described. Technical depth. The downstream systems. The banking integration layer. The LATAM regulatory environment.

She worked fast. She worked well.

Each question she drafted for the Sovren team was precise and necessary and moved the deal forward in the direction the deal was already moving.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. A push notification.

Tehran protest death toll in the thousands, opposition groups report. Most are under 30 years old. Security forces deny systematic use of force.

She read it at her desk with both hands still on the keyboard.

The uncle she had never met. The family name still in that city.

She put the phone face-down on the desk.

She worked through lunch.

The question list grew.

Fourteen items. Each one built to surface the specific technical depth Calder had described. She knew, constructing each question, that Walker would recognize what the questions were for. Walker would understand that someone on the other side of the table wanted more than the pitch. Walker would answer with the surgical precision she had used in the first meeting. Accurate and incomplete.

She thought about the credit union reel she had watched on the R train.

She thought about the reversal window.

Sovren wins. Every time.

She closed the question list and saved it.

· · ·

The afternoon arrived without announcement.

The IC onboarding memo for Garza was still open on her second screen. The certification block at the bottom, empty. The one field she could not fill with anything true.

By six the floor had emptied. Reception had been dark since four. The corner suite was still lit.

She did not pack her bag. The certification sat open where it had sat since nine.

At six-twenty-nine Calder came down the hall, coat over his arm.

He stopped at her desk.

“The Garza certification didn’t come through.”

“I can’t verify him.” Level. “I ran every registry, every database the firm pays for. There’s no entity behind the name. There’s nothing to certify.”

“The verification is Anderson Prescott.”

“The form doesn’t ask who referred him. It asks whether I verified his identity. I didn’t.”

“Then you put Prescott’s name in the field, and you sign it.” Still easy. Still warm. “We did this dance this morning.”

Something moved up the back of her neck. She kept both hands on the desk so they would not do anything she had not decided to let them do.

“He doesn’t exist.”

Her voice was not as level now and she heard it. “You know he doesn’t. There’s a man somewhere with that name and that money without one verifiable fact attached to him, and you want my name on the page that says I checked.”

For the first time he set the coat down. Over the back of the empty chair. Then he pulled the chair out and sat, which was worse than standing, because sitting meant he had time.

“Say the rest of it,” he said.

“I’m not signing it.”

It was out of her before she had decided to let it out. The room went very quiet and she felt it drop through her chest.

He did not get angry. The warmth simply vacated.

“All right,” he said. He folded his hands. “Then here is what happens. You don’t sign, you’ve made a decision, and I respect decisions. Tonight I call someone less careful than you, who signs it before nine without reading past the first page. The deal closes anyway. Nothing you’re afraid of stops being true, it only stops being yours to have flagged. And Monday I begin the quiet work of explaining to two partners that the analyst on the most important round of the year couldn’t be relied on when it counted. You’ll leave on your own inside a month with a reference that says nothing actionable and means everything, and you will carry it into every office in New York. Because Anderson Prescott’s name is in every office in New York.”

He let it sit.

“Or,” he said, the warmth returning, “you sign a form the way it gets signed forty times a year by people who sleep fine, and tomorrow is Thursday.”

Her hand had found the strap of her bag. She did not remember reaching for it. Standing was one motion away, the one that got her out of a room before the room could decide what she was. Detroit was in that motion. Everything she had built was the distance she’d put between herself and ever needing anyone in a room like this one.

And there was no one to call. No name in her phone that changed what nine in the morning looked like. The seat stood in for all of it. The seat was the proof that the distance was real. Stand up now and she did not lose a job. She lost the evidence.

Her hand came off the strap.

She turned to the screen because she could not keep her face arranged and look at him at the same time.

The onboarding memo was open to the certification block.

Verified identity. She typed the name into the field: Alejandro Garza. A name and nothing behind it. Background-check summary. She typed Principal reference: Anderson Prescott, Founding Partner. Her hand was not steady and she did not try to make it steady; there was nothing left over to spend on that.

The last line.

I certify that the above is accurate and complete. A box, and her name beside it.

She checked the box. She typed her own name into the line, the one true word on the form, sitting under the false ones.

Before the part of her that managed consequences could speak she pressed submit. A dialog asked her to confirm.

“Go ahead,” Calder said. Politely, without encouraging.

She confirmed.

Certification recorded. A green bar. The system took it without friction.

“Good.” He stood, lifted the coat off the chair. The warmth was fully back now, seamless, as if it had never gone, costing him exactly nothing.

“Get some sleep. Big week. This closes on schedule, you’re looking at VP by Q3.”

Three steps toward the elevator, he stopped. Half-turned. Whatever had started on her face, he caught the edge of it.

He chose not to see it.

The elevator opened.

She stayed in the office until the lights went dark on the floor of the building across the street.

Then the breath went in and would not come level, and a sound got out of her that she did not recognize as one she made. Low, guttural. She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth and gripped the desk edge with the other and came apart in the dark with the green bar still glowing. Fast. Total.

A minute. Less.

Then it passed.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She breathed until the breath went level. On the wall of the corner suite the portrait held the name she had put in the verification line, the man whose existence had never once required verifying.

She put on her coat.

The badge reader at the fourteenth-floor entrance of Prescott Calder registered green as she passed through.

It always did.

Chapter Fourteen–A — 902 Broadway Hotel, NYC · Early Morning EST

The hotel was three blocks from the presentation at 902 Broadway.

The suite faced east. The curtains were still open. An open laptop next to a picked-over room service breakfast.

Garza had been up since 4:45 AM.

A workout at 5:00 AM.

Showered. A Tom Ford suit.

On his laptop by 6:06 AM.

LATAM integration timelines. Regulatory mapping across Mexico and Colombia. Banking partner dossiers. All in Spanish. Six weeks of his own work.

The updated Prescott Calder package had come through yesterday. The requested materials with an extensive analyst memo signed A. Rostami. He had read it twice.

This morning’s email had an additional file. Four pages of unformatted text. Technical notes. English. No letterhead. He had already read it three times.

Detailed query topics for the presentation. Downstream system architecture. Behavioral inference capabilities. Regulatory compliance across LATAM jurisdictions.

Queries that did not sound like the language of an investor.

He had asked Victor once about the sender. It was not his concern. That had been the end of the conversation.

His phone rang at 9:02.

Country code \+58.

“Alejandro.”

Spanish from here. Cold. Surgical.

Questions. Updates. Instructions.

Garza listened.

Meeting confirmed. Ninety minutes. The analyst had prepared the materials and the room. The technical team would follow Walker’s lead.

The distribution structure had been discussed on the previous call.

His team’s relationships inside the Tier-1 and Tier-2 LATAM institutions. It would be presented as an opportunity, not a condition. The founder would see it as LATAM market access. The managing partner would see it as a larger deal. The capital commitment would increase to make the partnership attractive. Five million. Up from the original three-point-five.

He opened a Banco Santander México window on his laptop.

The Wednesday wire had cleared.

The balance on closing. Two hundred fifty thousand total, as agreed.

“Agreed.”

The call ended.

Garza closed the laptop and slid it into a Gucci leather bag along with the small flip phone.

He finished the breakfast coffee.

He put on the suit jacket and winter coat.

He left the suite at 9:34.
